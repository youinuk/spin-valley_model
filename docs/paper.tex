\begin{abstract}
We study charge-noise dephasing and valley leakage during coherent
electron spin shuttling in Si/SiGe under the synthetic spin--orbit
field of a periodic micromagnet array, a controlled reference geometry
motivated by proposed shuttling-bus architectures. A valley-aware
co-design program would seek
micromagnet geometry and shuttling trajectory that jointly suppress
both loss channels, but the outcome depends on an assumed effective
spin--valley coupling Hamiltonian $H_{sv}$ that is not uniquely
fixed. Rather than claiming a single robust operating basin, we report
a \emph{model-risk diagnostic}. We find that even the \emph{existence}
of a cross-ansatz robust operating point is convention-dependent: in a
stray-field-only Zeeman bookkeeping the staged search finds no robust
candidate surviving statistical strengthening, whereas in the
physically transparent total-field convention two edge-pocket
robust candidates ($v=10$\,m/s, well closer to the magnets) survive
targeted re-testing with 30 charge-noise realizations as robust in all four
ans\"atze ($4/4$) at the two coupling strengths; two other apparent
full-atlas candidates fail the same $\ge 3/4$ cross-ansatz re-test. Because no generally accepted local
$\lambda_{sv}(x)$ is yet available for a shuttled Si/SiGe dot in a
micromagnet field, these ans\"atze encode different plausible modeling
assumptions rather than a derived microscopic coupling. We then
quantify how strongly the coupling-ansatz choice controls the
conclusions with a sensitivity atlas over 432 model-conditions. The atlas does not rank absolute shuttling
performance; it ranks and classifies the \emph{baseline-relative
response} that the coupling induces in two loss channels (valley
leakage and dephasing). Within this response map, the \emph{ranking}
of operating conditions is largely conserved across ans\"atze (mean
Spearman $\rho \approx 0.84$). The \emph{thresholded classification},
which records the sign pattern of the above-threshold responses while
retaining single-channel and below-threshold cases as separate
categories, remains strongly model-dependent (mean exact-label
agreement $\approx 36\%$).
Agreement is lowest for the B$_z$--B$_x$ and A--B$_x$ pairs, both of
which set the $\partial_x B_x$-based ansatz against a
$\partial_x B_z$-based one. A further test isolates the Zeeman-energy
convention entering the resonance window. We recompute the atlas after
replacing the stray-field-only form by the physically transparent
total-field form, holding the four kernels, the condition grid, the
noise model, and the seeds fixed. The mean rank correlation shifts from
$0.68$ to $0.84$, and the run also changes which ansatz appears
anomalous and whether any robust candidate exists at all. A
bookkeeping-level choice therefore moves qualitative, design-level
conclusions. We conclude that, for this reference geometry and ansatz
family, calibration of the effective spin--valley coupling is a
prerequisite
before geometry-optimization claims can be trusted, and we provide the
atlas as a reusable
diagnostic. Absolute full-model error proxies must be reported
separately and are not a target of this study.
\end{abstract}

Coherent transport of electron spin qubits between distant sites
(``shuttling'') is a key enabler for scalable Si/SiGe spin-qubit
architectures~\cite{kunne_spinbus_2024,langrock_blueprint_2023,gonzalez-zalba_scaling_2021,veldhorst_silicon_2017,li_crossbar_2018,vandersypen_interfacing_2017},
allowing qubit registers to be spatially separated
from readout and control hardware. Silicon spin qubits have advanced
rapidly, with high-fidelity single- and two-qubit
gates~\cite{xue_quantum_2022,tanttu_assessment_2024,huang_high-fidelity_2024,noiri_shuttling-based_2022},
multi-qubit processors~\cite{philips_universal_2022,fernandez_de_fuentes_running_2026,thorvaldson_grovers_2025},
quantum error correction~\cite{takeda_quantum_2022}, and an
industrial fabrication path~\cite{meunier_silicon_2025,koch_industrial_2025,elsayed_low_2024};
single-spin physics and readout are by now
well established~\cite{hanson_spins_2007,burkard_semiconductor_2023,morello_single-shot_2010,hu_single-electron_2025}.
High-fidelity conveyor-mode and
bucket-brigade shuttling have been demonstrated experimentally in
Si/SiGe~\cite{de_smet_high-fidelity_2025,seidler_conveyor-mode_2022,mills_shuttling_2019},
shuttling-based two-qubit links and
entanglement distribution between separated registers have been realized
experimentally~\cite{noiri_shuttling-based_2022,matsumoto_two-qubit_2025,ademi_distributing_2025},
and gates acting directly on moving spins have been proposed
theoretically~\cite{fernandez-fernandez_spin-orbit-enabled_2025}.
A central obstacle is the valley
degree of freedom: the near-degeneracy of the two low-lying
conduction-band valleys in strained Si means that a shuttled electron
can leak into the excited valley, and the spatial variation of the
model valley-detuning landscape $E_v(x)$ along the shuttling path
couples motion to the valley sector; low-valley-splitting pockets arising from alloy
disorder are a known threat to shuttling
fidelity~\cite{paquelet_wuetz_atomic_2022,losert_strategies_2024,zwerver_shuttling_2023},
and recent shuttling-based maps report physical valley splittings from
$1.5$ to $200\,\mu$eV along real conveyor
trajectories, dominated by alloy disorder, with trajectory selection
around low-splitting regions demonstrated as a practical
mitigation~\cite{volmer_impact_2026}. When combined with a micromagnet-induced synthetic
spin--orbit field~\cite{unseld_baseband_2025,yoneda_quantum-dot_2018},
valley transitions become correlated with spin
dephasing, so that geometry and trajectory choices affect spin
coherence and valley leakage simultaneously.

Against this background we ask whether sensitivity-map conclusions in
such architectures are stable against plausible choices of the
unresolved microscopic spin--valley coupling profile.
A natural program is \emph{valley-aware co-design}:
jointly optimizing micromagnet geometry and shuttling trajectory to
find a robust operating regime that suppresses both valley leakage
and dephasing. Implicit in such optimization is a model of how the
spin and valley sectors couple---an effective spin--valley
Hamiltonian $H_{sv}$. The literature motivates several ingredients
for such a coupling: gradient-induced synthetic SOC, valley-pocket
localization, and spin--valley hot spots. It does not, however, provide
a unique local $\lambda_{sv}(x)$ profile for the shuttled-dot setting.

In this work we ask a prior question: \emph{how much do co-design
conclusions depend on the choice of effective coupling model?}
Figure~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes the setup, the four coupling
ans\"atze, the analysis pipeline, and the two headline diagnostics. We proceed in two steps rather than
reporting a single optimized operating point. First, a staged
falsification chain and a targeted higher-statistics re-test show that
whether a cross-ansatz robust operating point exists \emph{at all}
depends on the modeling convention. No candidate survives in the
stray-field bookkeeping, whereas two grid-local edge-pocket robust
candidates survive re-testing at $n_{\rm real}=30$ across all four
ans\"atze in the adopted total-field convention, where $n_{\rm real}$ is
the number of independent charge-noise realizations averaged per
condition. Second, we quantify
the residual model dependence with a sensitivity atlas.
Our central finding is a separation between two notions of
model dependence: the \emph{ranking} of operating conditions is
largely conserved across ans\"atze, whereas the \emph{physical
interpretation} (which thresholded response category each condition
receives) remains
strongly model-dependent, with the lowest agreement in the B$_z$--B$_x$
and A--B$_x$ pairs. A further finding of the
same kind arises one level below the ansatz choice. Correcting the
Zeeman-energy convention entering the resonance window alone moves the
headline statistics substantially. Modeling \emph{conventions}, and not
only ansatz selection, therefore change design-level conclusions.

\section{Related work and positioning}

Prior studies of valley physics in Si/SiGe have characterized valley
splitting statistics~\cite{paquelet_wuetz_atomic_2022,losert_practical_2023},
the role of interface disorder and valley--orbit
coupling~\cite{friesen_theory_2010,hosseinkhani_theory_2022}, and
shuttling-induced valley
excitation~\cite{langrock_blueprint_2023,losert_strategies_2024,volmer_mapping_2024,oda_suppressing_2026}. Work on micromagnet-based
spin manipulation~\cite{unseld_baseband_2025,yoneda_quantum-dot_2018} has
established synthetic
spin--orbit coupling as a control resource, and the role of
spin--orbit interaction and $g$-tensor anisotropy in quantum dots has
been studied in both electron and hole
systems~\cite{tanttu_controlling_2019,jin_probing_2024,geyer_anisotropic_2024,hendrickx_sweet-spot_2024}.
Charge-noise dephasing
during shuttling and in static dots has been modeled and measured with
filter-function and disorder-aware
approaches~\cite{langrock_blueprint_2023,struck_low-frequency_2020,krzywda_coherence_2026,burkard_semiconductor_2023,yoneda_noise-correlation_2023},
and hopping/baseband control with site-dependent quantization axes has
emerged as an alternative to resonant
driving~\cite{wang_operating_2024,unseld_baseband_2025}. Disorder and
strain effects on spin--orbit coupling have been quantified in related
Ge hole-spin device models~\cite{sarkar_effect_2025,mauro_strain_2025}.
Trajectory and device parameters for shuttling \emph{have} been
optimized in specific studies, including velocity and design trade-offs
in blueprint analyses and disorder-aware shuttling
strategies~\cite{langrock_blueprint_2023,losert_strategies_2024}.
Generic optimal-control machinery is also
available~\cite{khaneja_optimal_2005,niu_universal_2019}.

In such studies the effective coupling model is held fixed, which is the
right choice where the model is validated for the regime in question. The
setting we address is the local spin--valley coupling of a dot shuttled
through a micromagnet field. No generally validated local
$\lambda_{sv}(x)$ that can be adopted without additional modeling
assumptions is yet available for this setting (Sec.~\ref{sec:model}), so there the
model choice itself becomes a source of design risk. Quantifying that
risk, rather than optimizing under any one choice, is the purpose of
this work. Recent conveyor-mode
measurements increasingly constrain the relevant microscopic
ingredients. They provide spatial maps of the valley splitting along
shuttling trajectories and, together with valley-resolved $g$-factor
maps, constrain the complex intervalley
coupling~\cite{volmer_impact_2026,volmer_gfactor_2026}. These
measurements do not by themselves determine the effective local
moving-dot spin--valley kernel parameterized here. They sharpen the
experimental target that a future microscopic $\lambda_{sv}(x)$ must
fit. Valley-aware
co-design in the sense used here means jointly tuning geometry and
trajectory against both valley leakage and dephasing. It is accordingly
a prospective program rather than an established literature.

Our contribution is orthogonal and methodological. Instead of optimizing
under one model, we benchmark four spatial-profile ans\"atze for
$\lambda_{sv}(x)$ against each other and quantify the sensitivity of
the resulting conclusions. Each ansatz is a phenomenological kernel
multiplying one fixed minimal spin--valley operator. Micromagnets and electron shuttling are individually
established experimentally~\cite{yoneda_quantum-dot_2018,unseld_baseband_2025,de_smet_high-fidelity_2025,seidler_conveyor-mode_2022},
but periodically patterned micromagnet shuttling architectures remain
largely proposed or emerging
designs~\cite{kunne_spinbus_2024,langrock_blueprint_2023,unseld_baseband_2025}; we
accordingly treat the periodic array as a controlled reference geometry
for the diagnostic comparison. Whether a robust candidate exists is itself convention- and
ansatz-dependent. That result and the diagnostic atlas together
identify the next bottleneck as calibration of the effective
spin--valley coupling model, not another pass of geometry optimization
alone.

\section{Model}
\label{sec:model}

We consider an electron shuttled at constant velocity $v$ along $x$
through a periodic micromagnet array, with a position-dependent
diabatic valley detuning
%
\begin{equation}
E_v(x)=E_{v,\rm base}-\left(E_{v,\rm base}-E_{v,\min}\right)
\exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-x_c)^2}{2\sigma_p^2}\right],
\label{eq:evx}
\end{equation}
%
a baseline with a Gaussian pocket (minimum) near the shuttling path, using
$E_{v,\rm base}=100\,\mu$eV, $E_{v,\min}=5\,\mu$eV and
$\sigma_p=30$\,nm; for A$_{\rm pocket}$ we set
$\sigma_\lambda=\sigma_p$. The constant-velocity trajectory spans
$x\in[x_c-4\sigma_p,\,x_c+4\sigma_p]$, a total path length of
$240$\,nm. We use this detuning landscape as a phenomenological
surrogate for the measured spatial variation of the physical valley
splitting, which is expected from alloy-disorder and interface
physics~\cite{langrock_blueprint_2023,paquelet_wuetz_atomic_2022,losert_practical_2023},
and has recently been mapped directly along a shuttling path by
conveyor-mode spin-coherent
measurements~\cite{volmer_mapping_2024}. Representative values for the
device geometry, valley energetics, and shuttling velocity are
summarized in Table~\ref{tab:params}.

We model the spin and valley degrees of freedom with a minimal
four-level effective Hamiltonian
%
\begin{align}
H(x) = \tfrac{1}{2}g\mu_B [B_{\rm ext}+B_z(x)]\,\sigma_z\otimes I_v
      + \tfrac{1}{2}g\mu_B B_x(x)\,\sigma_x\otimes I_v \nonumber\\
      + \tfrac{1}{2}\varepsilon_v(x)\,I_s\otimes\tau_z
      + \Delta_v\,I_s\otimes\tau_x
      + \lambda_{sv}(x)\,\sigma_x\otimes\tau_x,
\end{align}
%
where $\sigma_i$ and $\tau_i$ are Pauli operators on the spin and
valley two-level spaces, $B_z(x)$ and $B_x(x)$ are the longitudinal and
transverse components of the \emph{micromagnet stray field}, and
$B_{\rm ext}=0.5$\,T is the uniform external field along $z$. The spin
block is the Zeeman Hamiltonian in the total longitudinal field
$B_{\rm ext}+B_z(x)$ together with the micromagnet transverse field; the
transverse term $g\mu_B B_x(x)$ is a
position-dependent transverse Zeeman \emph{energy}, not an AC EDSR
drive. Because $B_{\rm ext}$ is spatially uniform it drops out of every
spatial derivative, so the gradient-based ans\"atze below use the
micromagnet stray-field gradients $\partial_x B_z(x)$ and
$\partial_x B_x(x)$. The valley block is a generic two-level avoided-crossing model
with diabatic detuning $\varepsilon_v(x)$ and off-diagonal coupling
$\Delta_v$, so its isolated eigenlevel splitting is
$E_{\rm gap}(x)=\sqrt{\varepsilon_v(x)^2+4\Delta_v^2}$. Over the
$5$--$100\,\mu$eV input range used here this exceeds $\varepsilon_v(x)$
by less than $2\%$, with the largest relative difference at the pocket
minimum and less than $0.02\%$ near the $E_v\simeq E_Z$ crossings that
set the proxy hot spots. The diabatic input $\varepsilon_v(x)$ is the
landscape $E_v(x)$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:evx}, whose symbol we keep to match
the released code and data; it should therefore not be read as the
dressed eigenlevel gap. We treat
$\Delta_v$ as a phenomenological mixing parameter; microscopically, such intervalley coupling arises
from interface and valley--orbit physics~\cite{friesen_theory_2010}. This Hamiltonian is a diagnostic effective model,
not a microscopically derived Si/SiGe $H_{sv}$: the term
$\lambda_{sv}\sigma_x\otimes\tau_x$ is a minimal real
spin-flip/valley-flip Pauli-product ansatz (one of several possible
structures, e.g.\ $\sigma_x\tau_y$ or $\sigma_y\tau_x$), and we do not
claim it to be a unique microscopic form. All four ans\"atze below
share this single operator structure and differ only in the scalar
spatial profile $\lambda_{sv}(x)$: we compare four phenomenological
spatial kernels multiplying one fixed minimal operator, not four
microscopic Hamiltonians. Microscopically, spin--valley
mixing follows from matrix elements of intrinsic and synthetic
spin--orbit terms between valley-dependent envelope
states~\cite{hosseinkhani_theory_2022,huang_spin_2014}.

The coupling strength is set by a profile $\lambda_{sv}(x)$ whose form
is itself uncertain. Rather than assume a single profile, we compare
four \emph{phenomenological} toy-level ans\"atze. These are motivated
by known ingredients but are not derived from them (spatial
Zeeman-energy variation from the micromagnet
gradients~\cite{langrock_blueprint_2023,struck_low-frequency_2020},
transverse-gradient synthetic spin--orbit
coupling~\cite{borjans_single-spin_2019,zhang_controlling_2021,hosseinkhani_theory_2022},
valley-pocket
localization~\cite{paquelet_wuetz_atomic_2022,losert_strategies_2024,volmer_mapping_2024},
and the physical spin--valley hot spot associated with near-degeneracy
of the dressed spin and valley
sectors~\cite{yang_spin-valley_2013,huang_spin_2014}, which the released
kernels represent by the input-detuning proxy
$E_v(x)\simeq E_Z(x)$). We use
the physically transparent total-field convention throughout, in which
the Zeeman energy is evaluated with the full local field,
%
\begin{equation}
E_Z(x) = g\mu_B\,[\,B_{\rm ext} + B_z(x)\,],
\label{eq:ez}
\end{equation}
%
with $B_{\rm ext}=0.5$\,T (Table~\ref{tab:params}). The four ans\"atze
are then
%
\begin{align}
& \text{A:} \quad \lambda_{sv}(x) = \lambda_0\,
  \frac{|\partial_x B_z(x)|}{\max_x|\partial_x B_z|}, \\
& \text{A}_{\rm pocket}\text{:} \quad \lambda_{sv}(x) = \lambda_{sv}^{\rm A}(x)\,
  \exp\!\left[-\frac{(x-x_c)^2}{2\sigma_\lambda^2}\right], \\
& \text{B}_z\text{:} \quad \lambda_{sv}(x) = \lambda_{sv}^{\rm A}(x)\,
  \exp\!\left[-\frac{(E_v(x)-E_Z(x))^2}{2\sigma_E^2}\right], \\
& \text{B}_x\text{:} \quad \lambda_{sv}(x) = \lambda_0\,
  \frac{|\partial_x B_x(x)|}{\max_x|\partial_x B_x|}\,
  \exp\!\left[-\frac{(E_v(x)-E_Z(x))^2}{2\sigma_E^2}\right],
\end{align}
%
where $E_Z(x)$ is the local Zeeman energy of Eq.~\eqref{eq:ez}, so that
the diagnostic condition $E_v(x)\simeq E_Z(x)$ produces two flank proxy
crossings
around the pocket. (Under an alternative bookkeeping convention, the window is evaluated
with a stray-field-only Zeeman energy, which shifts the resonance
windows toward the pocket bottom and, as we show in
Sec.~\ref{sec:results}, moves the atlas statistics substantially. This
convention sensitivity is itself part of the model risk we report.)

A
is a longitudinal-gradient diagnostic baseline, motivated by the
spatial Zeeman-energy variation the micromagnet imprints rather than
by a microscopic spin-flip coupling.
A$_{\rm pocket}$ is a spatial-localization ansatz that weights the
gradient coupling by a Gaussian envelope centred on the low-$E_v$
pocket. B$_z$ is a longitudinal-gradient resonance stress test rather than a
literature-derived spin--valley matrix element. B$_z$ and B$_x$ are
resonance-window ans\"atze motivated by the spin--valley hot
spot~\cite{yang_spin-valley_2013,huang_spin_2014}. Their Gaussian
windows are centred on the input-detuning condition
$E_v(x)\simeq E_Z(x)$ and are diagnostic proxies for the dressed
crossing rather than microscopic resonance line shapes. They use two
alternative choices of
magnetic-gradient component: the longitudinal gradient $\partial_x B_z$
sets the local Zeeman-splitting variation (the dephasing
gradient~\cite{langrock_blueprint_2023,struck_low-frequency_2020}), whereas the
transverse gradient $\partial_x B_x$ is the component that drives spin
flips in slanting-field
EDSR~\cite{tokura_coherent_2006,pioro-ladriere_electrically_2008,zhang_controlling_2021}. Neither
choice is prescribed for a shuttled dot by the literature, which is
precisely the model uncertainty probed here.

None of the four profiles should be
interpreted as a literature-standard or microscopically derived
spin--valley coupling; the Gaussian resonance window
$e^{-(E_v-E_Z)^2/(2\sigma_E^2)}$ in particular is a regularized
diagnostic proxy for the hot-spot enhancement, not a standard
expression. They are diagnostic kernels used to quantify how strongly
the conclusions depend on plausible, non-unique modeling choices.

One might ask whether the gradient-only kernels A and
A$_{\rm pocket}$ are physically admissible, given the known
spin--valley enhancement near $E_v\approx E_Z$. In the diabatic
basis of the Hamiltonian above, the operator
$\lambda_{sv}(x)\,\sigma_x\otimes\tau_x$ couples a pair of states whose
uncoupled energy difference is $E_v(x)-E_Z(x)$. A nonzero
off-diagonal coupling at a flank crossing therefore produces an
avoided crossing and resonantly enhanced spin--valley mixing through
the four-level dynamics, even when $\lambda_{sv}(x)$ contains no
explicit resonance window~\cite{yang_spin-valley_2013,huang_spin_2014}.

For the profiles considered here, A and A$_{\rm pocket}$ remain
nonzero at the relevant flank crossings. The window in B$_z$/B$_x$
tests the distinct, stronger phenomenological assumption that the
effective off-diagonal coupling amplitude is itself localized near
resonance, thereby adding profile localization to the dynamical
level-crossing enhancement. Microscopic spin--valley matrix elements
depend on spin--orbit and field-gradient matrix elements between
valley-dependent envelope states and need not take the particular
Gaussian form in $E_v-E_Z$ used
here~\cite{hosseinkhani_theory_2022,huang_spin_2014}. The A-type
kernels should therefore be read as diagnostic detuning-independent
profiles, not as uniquely derived microscopic matrix elements. Neither
family is a null straw man; together they bracket complementary
assumptions about the spatial structure of the same off-diagonal
coupling.

Figure~\ref{fig:profiles} shows the four profiles for a representative
pocket: the gradient-only model A is delocalized over the whole
period, A$_{\rm pocket}$ concentrates the gradient coupling near the
pocket, and the resonance models B$_z$/B$_x$ are sharply peaked where
$E_v(x)$ crosses $E_Z(x)$. An important subtlety arises because
all three of A$_{\rm pocket}$, B$_z$, and B$_x$ \emph{multiply the
field gradient} $|\partial_x B_z|$ (or $|\partial_x B_x|$). The two gradient components are set by the array
alone, not by the pocket. By symmetry $B_z$ is extremal and $B_x$
vanishes at the array-symmetry point $x=0$, so $|\partial_x B_z|$ has a
node there; for the fitted baseline field $|\partial_x B_x|$ also attains
its maximum at that point. The centred-pocket configuration places
$x_c=0$ there as well, so the node--pocket alignment is a placement
choice rather than a generic property of a centred valley pocket.
Consequently
A$_{\rm pocket}=|\partial_x B_z|\times$Gaussian is
\emph{zero at the pocket centre} and forms two symmetric peaks on the
flanks rather than a single peak at the centre. A$_{\rm pocket}$ is thus
``pocket-localized'' in its Gaussian envelope but inherits the
gradient's flank structure; only when the pocket sits off-centre (where
the gradient no longer vanishes at the well bottom) does its weight
become strongly asymmetric and single-flank dominated. The resonance
windows (B$_z$, B$_x$)
activate symmetrically on both flanks for the centred pocket as well;
the profiles therefore differ not in which side of the pocket they
occupy but in their \emph{shape, width, phase relative to the driving
gradient, and amplitude}. In particular, because B$_x$ is built from
$\partial_x B_x$ rather than $\partial_x B_z$, and the two gradient
components are approximately phase shifted, its weight peaks slightly
outside the
crossing where the $\partial_x B_z$-based kernels peak slightly inside
it. This offset is consistent with the distinct
thresholded-classification behavior that B$_x$ shows in the atlas. (A
left/right asymmetry appears only for an off-centred pocket, where
the two flank crossings are inequivalent; the centred-pocket profiles
used here are two-sided and symmetric, and any single-sided
``opposite side'' reading of them is an argmax tie-break artifact of
exactly degenerate twin peaks.)

We emphasize that $\Delta_v$ and $\sigma_E$ are toy parameters of the
diagnostic model rather than measured constants; the geometric and
velocity baselines, by contrast, are representative of reported
Si/SiGe shuttling devices.

The observables target the two loss channels that the literature
identifies as central to Si/SiGe shuttling: valley excitation
(leakage) and valley-induced spin
dephasing~\cite{losert_strategies_2024,de_smet_high-fidelity_2025,volmer_mapping_2024,oda_suppressing_2026}.
We quantify them as the change in valley-leakage probability
$\Delta P_v$ and the change in dephasing phase variance
$\Delta\chi_\phi$ induced by the spin--valley coupling. We use
three nested models: a spin-only reference with charge noise but no
valley sector ($\mathrm{M1}$), a valley-aware reference without charge
noise ($\mathrm{M1V}$), and the full model with both charge noise and
valley coupling ($\mathrm{M2}$). The two responses are isolated against
\emph{different} references by design, each separating one
cross-effect: $\Delta P_v = P_v^{\mathrm{M2}}-P_v^{\mathrm{M1V}}$ (the
effect of charge noise on valley leakage) and
$\Delta\chi_\phi = \chi^{\mathrm{M2}}-\chi^{\mathrm{M1}}$ (the effect
of the valley sector on charge-noise dephasing); the dephasing is
computed within a filter-function description of $1/f$ charge
noise~\cite{burkard_semiconductor_2023,struck_low-frequency_2020,viola_dynamical_1999}.
Thus $\mathcal{R} = (\Delta P_v, \Delta\chi_\phi)$ is not a
decomposition of a single infidelity but a pair of cross-effect
measures. $\Delta P_v$ and $\Delta\chi_\phi$ are
\emph{baseline-relative response diagnostics}, not absolute error
rates. Absolute performance would be read from the full-model values
$P_v^{\mathrm{M2}}$ and $\chi_\phi^{\mathrm{M2}}$ (or a derived
phase-error proxy $p_\phi=(1-e^{-\chi_\phi/2})/2$), which we do not
optimize here.

Under the standard assumption that each component is
monotonic in the infidelity of its channel (larger leakage and larger
dephasing both reduce fidelity), we classify each condition by the
sign of each \emph{above-threshold} response. A condition is
\emph{robust} when both responses exceed their channel floors and improve
($\Delta P_v<0$ and $\Delta\chi_\phi<0$), \emph{valley-trade} or
\emph{spin-trade} when both exceed their floors with opposite signs, and
\emph{both-worsen} when both exceed their floors and worsen, using
effect-size floors $|\Delta P_v|\ge 10^{-4}$,
$|\Delta\chi_\phi|\ge 10^{-3}$.
The realization count $n_{\rm real}$ introduced above counts Monte Carlo
repetitions of the simulated noise, not experimental repetitions, and is
the column name used in the released data tables and scripts. These floors are not taken from an
external fidelity standard, and they are not statistical-significance
cutoffs or Monte Carlo confidence bounds. They are operational criteria
that define a dead zone on channel-appropriate response scales; the decade
offset between $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-3}$ reflects that $|\Delta\chi_\phi|$
is typically about an order of magnitude larger than $|\Delta P_v|$ in
our data. Two separate checks probe different sensitivities. A threshold
sweep in the Supplementary Material tests dependence on the floor choice.
Candidate stability is assessed separately by re-running the four screened
$\ge 3/4$ candidates at $n_{\rm real}=30$ (Sec.~\ref{sec:results}), which
is where individual low-statistics classifications are tested. When only one channel is above
threshold,
the condition is placed in a separate single-channel category rather
than forced into a two-channel quadrant; this avoids contaminating the
\emph{both-worsen} class with one-channel cases. The classification
uses only the signs of the above-threshold responses and is therefore
insensitive to their relative scale; the rank correlation, by
contrast, uses the response magnitude
$|\mathcal{R}|=\sqrt{\Delta P_v^2+\Delta\chi_\phi^2}$, whose ordering
is preserved under uniform rescaling but does depend on the relative
weighting of the two channels (we report this dependence as a
robustness check).

A co-design decision reads off the
direction of the channel responses (whether each channel improves or
worsens), not only their magnitude. We therefore treat the four
two-channel quadrants as the decision-facing sign diagnostic and the
ranking as a
complementary magnitude-ordering diagnostic. The ranking remains
informative, but the two diagnostics answer different questions and
exhibit different degrees of model dependence.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{A staged falsification chain}

A central methodological point is that we do not assert a robust
basin from small-sample evidence. We first ran a six-stage
verification chain, in the original stray-field convention, in which
each stage either strengthens statistics or adds a coupling model, and
we tracked whether cross-validated robust candidates survive
(Table~\ref{tab:chain}). In that convention no candidate survived; as
we show below, however, the outcome of this same procedure
\emph{changes} under the adopted total-field convention, which is
itself one of our results.

The stage-5 re-test raises the statistics to $n_{\rm real}=30$. Every
candidate identified in the low-statistics stages ($n_{\rm real}=2$--$5$)
then fails the criterion \emph{in the stray-field convention}: none of
the earlier detections survives the strengthened statistics. Extending to the four-ans\"atze family
(stage 6; A, A$_{\rm pocket}$, B$_z$, B$_x$) evaluates 72 model rows;
among the 12 positive-$\lambda$ physical settings, none is robust in
$\ge 3/4$ ans\"atze in that convention. This staged search motivated
reframing the study from basin discovery to a coupling-ansatz
sensitivity atlas. As we show in Sec.~\ref{sec:results}, repeating the
$n_{\rm real}=30$ re-test in the adopted total-field convention
reverses the stage-5/6 conclusion for two grid-local candidates. We
therefore present the atlas, together with the convention- and
ansatz-dependence of the robust/fragile verdict, rather than a single
basin claim.

\subsection{Sensitivity atlas}

We use two pocket placements: $x_c=0$ for the centred case, aligned
with the array-symmetry point, and $x_c=25$\,nm for the off-centred edge
case.
On a grid of geometries (nine unique points: the baseline plus one
additive $+$ and $-$ step in each of four geometry parameters,
\texttt{period} ($\pm30$\,nm), \texttt{depth} ($\pm10$\,nm),
\texttt{half\_x} ($\pm5$\,nm), and \texttt{half\_z} ($\pm3$\,nm),
i.e.\ $1+4\times2=9$), velocities, and coupling
strengths $\lambda$, we compute six metrics. Here \texttt{depth} is the
signed vertical coordinate of the quantum well relative to the magnet
plane, $-50$\,nm at baseline; a $+$ step moves the well toward the magnet
array, reducing the magnet--well separation and strengthening the stray
field. The metrics are (i) per-model response
amplitude; (ii) raw geometry sensitivity
$S_\theta = |\mathcal{R}(\theta+\Delta\theta)
- \mathcal{R}(\theta-\Delta\theta)|/2\Delta\theta$;
(iii) model distance $D_{m_1 m_2}$ (raw and channel-balanced);
(iv) pairwise Spearman rank correlation of $|\mathcal{R}|$;
(v) classification agreement, the fraction of conditions assigned the
same thresholded response label, drawn from nine categories: the four
two-channel quadrants (\emph{robust}, \emph{valley-trade},
\emph{spin-trade}, \emph{both-worsen}), four single-channel categories,
and \emph{below-threshold}. We reserve ``quadrant'' for the four
two-channel labels; the released pipeline and the Fig.~\ref{fig:atlas}
panel titles retain the shorthand ``quadrant agreement'' for this
metric; (vi) relative geometry sensitivity (normalized by
$\theta_0$). The $\lambda=0$ slice is retained only as an invariance
check (separable limit, $\Delta\mathcal{R}\approx 0$). Realizations
share common random numbers across models to reduce comparison
variance. The reported rank and classification diagnostics are empirical
summaries over the fixed 432-condition grid.

The distinction between metrics (iv) and (v) is central to our
conclusions and is illustrated in Fig.~\ref{fig:quadrant}: two models
may rank a set of conditions by response \emph{magnitude} almost
identically (high rank correlation) while still assigning many
conditions to \emph{different} categories (low classification
agreement). Ranking captures ``how large is the response,'' whereas the
thresholded label records which response signs are resolved above the
stated floors. Within that label set, the four two-channel quadrants
capture the sign pattern of the baseline-relative responses, which is one
ingredient of a co-design decision; a final performance decision would
also require the absolute full-model leakage and dephasing proxies,
which we do not optimize here.

\paragraph{Ranking vs.\ interpretation.}
Over the 432 model-conditions of the full validation grid (centred and
edge pockets, $v\in\{5,10,20\}$\,m/s, $n_{\rm real}=5$; executed as a
case split and merged), the mean rank correlation is $\rho=0.84$
[Fig.~\ref{fig:atlas}(1)], with all six pairwise values in a narrow
high band ($0.79$--$0.91$). Condition ranking is therefore
\emph{largely conserved} across the ans\"atze. In contrast, the mean
classification agreement is only $36\%$ [Fig.~\ref{fig:atlas}(2)], with
pairwise values spanning $29\%$--$45\%$: the physical interpretation
(which category a condition falls into) is substantially more
model-dependent than the ranking. The separation between a
well-conserved magnitude ordering and a poorly-conserved thresholded
classification is the central quantitative result, and it is present
even though the ranking itself is high. Two checks address the two most
obvious alternative explanations: skewed category marginals and the
inclusion of non-quadrant labels.
Chance-corrected Cohen's $\kappa$ stays modest for all six pairs
($0.135$--$0.334$), so the low raw agreement is not simply a consequence
of skewed category marginals, of which \emph{below-threshold} is the
largest. And restricting the comparison to the conditions that both
ans\"atze place in a two-channel quadrant --- removing the
below-threshold and single-channel categories entirely --- leaves
agreement at $44.8\%$ ($47$ of $105$ such comparisons). That conditional
subset is small and selected, but it shows that low agreement is not
confined to the excluded categories.

\paragraph{Where the dependence lives.}
The residual model dependence is not uniform across pairs, and it now
lives almost entirely in the thresholded classification rather than in
the ranking. The two lowest-agreement pairs both set the
$\partial_x B_x$-based model against a $\partial_x B_z$-based model:
B$_z$ vs.\ B$_x$ at $29\%$ and A vs.\ B$_x$ at $30\%$. The two
$\partial_x B_z$-based models A and B$_z$ agree best ($45\%$
classification agreement, $\rho=0.91$). Even for the pair with the smallest overall
model distance, A vs.\ A$_{\rm pocket}$, individual conditions still
change quadrant [Fig.~\ref{fig:atlas}(5)]. B$_x$ is not, however, a
uniform outlier: the third B$_x$ pair, A$_{\rm pocket}$ vs.\ B$_x$,
is comparatively high ($44\%$); this is consistent with the
pocket-localized A$_{\rm pocket}$ sharing B$_x$'s concentration near the
pocket flanks.
The decisive modeling axis is therefore the \emph{gradient component}
entering the synthetic coupling ($\partial_x B_z$ vs.\
$\partial_x B_x$), modulated by spatial localization, rather than the
presence of a resonance window per se.
Under the total-field convention the rank correlations of the
B$_x$ pairs ($0.79$--$0.85$) are comparable to the others: the
legacy-convention appearance of a \emph{ranking} divergence for
B$_x$ was itself an artifact of the stray-field-only Zeeman
bookkeeping, and only the classification-level discrepancy survives the
correction. This is direct evidence that the interpretive conclusion
is controlled by modeling choices, here both the ansatz and the Zeeman
convention, rather than by the data.

\paragraph{Convention sensitivity as primary evidence.}
The Zeeman-energy convention entering the resonance window is not a
detail. Recomputing the entire atlas after replacing the
stray-field-only Zeeman energy with the total-field form
moves the mean rank correlation from $0.68$ to
$0.84$ and the mean classification agreement from $29\%$ to $36\%$, and it
dissolves the apparent B$_x$ ranking divergence noted above. Different equations trivially produce different
numbers. What matters here is what the comparison holds fixed and what
it flips. The four kernels, the 108-condition physical grid, the noise
model, and every random seed are identical between the two runs. Only
the bookkeeping of how the external field enters the hot-spot window
changes. Yet the outputs that flip are categorical rather than
marginal: which ansatz appears to be the outlier, and (below) whether
any cross-ansatz robust point exists. Neither flip is
predictable a priori from ``the equations differ.'' This one-variable comparison captures the
paper's thesis: design-level conclusions about valley-aware shuttling
are sensitive to modeling assumptions that current literature does not
fix. Physically, only the total-field
convention corresponds to the actual experimental situation
($B_{\rm ext}$ is present); we therefore adopt it for all headline
numbers and retain the stray-field run as an archived case study of how
a plausible bookkeeping slip propagates to design conclusions.

\paragraph{Convention-dependent existence of robust candidates.}
The convention change also flips a stronger, discrete conclusion:
whether any cross-ansatz robust operating point exists at all. In the
stray-field convention the staged chain (Table~\ref{tab:chain}) found
none surviving the strengthened statistics. In the adopted total-field atlas,
re-classifying the full $n_{\rm real}=5$ grid yields four physical
conditions robust in $\ge 3/4$ ans\"atze (one in $4/4$). We subjected
exactly those four to the same targeted re-test used in stage 5, now at
$n_{\rm real}=30$ and in the total-field convention
(Table~\ref{tab:retest}). Two no longer meet the $\ge 3/4$ cross-ansatz
criterion: the centred-pocket point at $v=5$\,m/s remains robust in $1/4$
ans\"atze and the edge point at $v=20$\,m/s in $2/4$, so their
low-statistics cross-ansatz classifications do not survive. \emph{Two survive as
robust in all four ans\"atze}: the edge-pocket condition at
$v=10$\,m/s with the reduced magnet--well separation, at both coupling
strengths
$\lambda\in\{0.5,1.0\}\,\mu$eV. A small grid-local
cross-ansatz robust candidate set therefore does exist in the
physically preferred convention. Its
identification is nonetheless model-contingent: it is invisible in the
stray-field bookkeeping, and even within the total-field convention two
of the four screening candidates fail the higher-statistics cross-ansatz
criterion. The surviving pair is itself grid-local and floor-based rather
than confidence-certified: its narrowest channel clears the
$|\Delta\chi_\phi|$ floor by only about $5\%$. The atlas's role is to flag which candidates
warrant this higher-statistics validation. It also exposes that the
robust/fragile verdict, and even the existence of a robust point,
depends on the coupling convention and ansatz family, not on the
geometry search alone.

\paragraph{Geometry sensitivity.}
Per nanometer of knob variation, all four models share the raw ordering
\texttt{half\_z} $>$ \texttt{half\_x} $>$ \texttt{depth} $>$
\texttt{period} (with A$_{\rm pocket}$ swapping
the top two): the magnet thickness dominates and the array period is the
weakest per-nm knob. This shared picture does not survive rescaling to
\emph{fractional} knob changes [Fig.~\ref{fig:atlas}(4b)]: the
per-fraction hierarchy differs between ans\"atze. Depth leads for A,
A$_{\rm pocket}$, and B$_x$, whereas for B$_z$ the \emph{period} becomes
the single most sensitive knob; a fractional period change rescales the
stray-field amplitude at the quantum-well depth roughly as
$e^{-2\pi z/a}$ and thereby shifts the resonance crossings to which
B$_z$ responds. The least-sensitive knob likewise varies. The
geometry-sensitivity hierarchy is therefore itself
ansatz-dependent once knob scales are accounted for, and we caution
against any absolute knob-importance claim; this extends the model-risk
message from the operating-condition verdicts to the geometry story
itself.

\section{Discussion}

The results indicate that conclusions about valley-aware
geometry/trajectory co-design are sensitive to the assumed effective
$H_{sv}$, particularly to the choice of magnetic-field gradient
component and to the Zeeman-energy convention. The four two-channel
quadrants form the decision-facing sign diagnostic: they distinguish
robust, trade-off, and both-worsen responses. This is also the diagnostic
we find to be most model-dependent. We do not conclude that robust operating points cannot
exist: in the physically preferred total-field convention two
grid-local edge-pocket candidates survive $n_{\rm real}=30$ re-testing
across all four ans\"atze.
The \emph{identification} of such a point is instead model-contingent:
the same staged procedure returns ``no robust candidate'' in one
convention and ``a surviving robust candidate'' in another.
Robust-region claims built on a single toy coupling and a single
convention are therefore not trustworthy without cross-checks.

The thresholded classification verdict is strongly coupling-dependent (mean
raw cross-ansatz
classification agreement $\approx36\%$; pairwise Cohen's
$\kappa=0.135$--$0.334$ on the same label set), and this dependence persists across the whole geometry
grid we scanned rather than averaging out along it. Even the
geometry-sensitivity hierarchy itself is ansatz-dependent once knob
scales are accounted for [Fig.~\ref{fig:atlas}(4b)]. A
geometry/trajectory optimization over the grid examined here therefore
does not remove the coupling-induced ambiguity in the verdict. For this co-design decision
the coupling must be constrained \emph{before} the geometry search can
be trusted. Orbital admixture, valley--orbit interaction, and
$g$-factor
anisotropy~\cite{friesen_theory_2010,hosseinkhani_theory_2022,zhang_controlling_2021,tanttu_controlling_2019}
are the quantities that decide the outcome here. Existing literature
addresses the relevant microscopic structure, and geometry remains an
essential design variable. Our narrower, data-supported result is that
the verdict is governed by the coupling model in a way the geometry
grid does not resolve.

The value of this comparison is conditional on a specific fact: no
generally validated effective local spin--valley kernel is currently
available for the specific \emph{shuttled}-dot/micromagnet regime
considered here. Were such a
model in hand one would simply adopt it and co-design directly. The
family would collapse to the single true coupling, and the
model-dependence we report would be moot. Design decisions today must
instead be made under non-unique phenomenological proxies, so the
disagreement between those proxies matters. A single proxy cannot
expose it, however realistic-looking, because model dependence is
visible only by \emph{comparing} a family.

The atlas's content is not merely that four deliberately different
kernels produce four different numbers; that much is true by construction. The informative output is
the \emph{structure} of the disagreement. Magnitude orderings are
largely conserved across the family while sign-level classifications
are not, so the fragile quantity is the one a design decision consumes.
The disagreement also concentrates on an identifiable physical axis,
the gradient component modulated by localization, rather than being
diffuse. The location and the asymmetry of this fragility do not follow
from the kernels being different. The toy family is therefore the
appropriate tool for a diagnostic of model sensitivity \emph{under
present ignorance}. This model-risk result characterizes the current
modeling problem for this reference geometry and diagnostic ansatz
family: the thresholded classification, including its two-channel sign
interpretation, is fragile across the family.
Microscopically informed valley landscapes, such as those constrained by
alloy-disorder calculations and conveyor-mode
measurements~\cite{paquelet_wuetz_atomic_2022,losert_practical_2023,volmer_mapping_2024},
can be incorporated into the same atlas, and a microscopic spin--valley
coupling can be added as a further member once one is validated for this
setting. We
expect them to narrow, but not by themselves collapse, the spread until
one is actually validated for this setting.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
All conclusions are scoped to four toy-level effective coupling
profiles, one fixed minimal spin--valley operator, one fixed
charge-noise description, and the periodic micromagnet reference
geometry examined here. The four ans\"atze differ only in their scalar
spatial profiles, so operator-level alternatives are outside the present
scope. The same
$1/f$ spectrum and a single representative, non-device-calibrated
displacement-noise amplitude enter both responses, since
$\Delta P_v$ measures the effect of charge noise on valley leakage and
$\Delta\chi_\phi$ the effect of the valley sector on charge-noise
dephasing. What is quantified here is therefore coupling-profile
uncertainty conditional on that noise description; noise-spectrum and
amplitude uncertainty are not scanned and form a separate axis.

The thresholded labels are operational classifications under the stated
effect-size floors, not confidence-certified assignments. The threshold
sweep and the targeted $n_{\rm real}=30$ re-test address two distinct
questions: sensitivity to the floor choice, and stability of the four
screened candidates.

The robust/fragile statements above therefore apply to the four
diagnostic ans\"atze and to the 432 model-conditions of the full
validation grid (centred and edge pockets, $v\in\{5,10,20\}$\,m/s,
$n_{\rm real}=5$), executed as a per-case split and merged offline.
Natural extensions are to measured or finite-device micromagnet fields,
to families of noise descriptions, and to microscopically derived
spin--valley couplings and operator structures; we leave these to future
work because the purpose here is to isolate coupling-profile dependence
against a fixed geometric backdrop.

\paragraph{Implications for co-design.}
Two practical implications follow. First, knob-importance claims depend
on both the coupling ansatz and the normalization used for a geometry
change, so no absolute statement survives scale accounting. Such claims
should state which ansatz produced them and whether sensitivity is
reported per absolute or per fractional variation.
Second, because both the full thresholded classification and the
two-channel sign interpretation are model-dependent and concentrated
on the gradient component, any claim that a particular operating point
is ``robust'' should be accompanied by a statement of which coupling
model produced it. Proposed mitigations such as varying the shuttling
velocity, dot shape, or path~\cite{losert_strategies_2024} should
likewise be assessed across coupling ans\"atze, or microscopic coupling models, rather than within one.
The decisive next step is to calibrate the
gradient structure of the synthetic coupling against a microscopic
model or experiment, rather than to refine the geometry optimizer alone.

\section{Conclusion}

We have presented a sensitivity atlas for phenomenological
spin--valley coupling ans\"atze in Si/SiGe shuttling. The atlas does
not rank absolute shuttling performance; it ranks and classifies the
baseline-relative response that the coupling induces, and asks how
stable those rankings and classifications are across ans\"atze. The
methodological contribution is twofold. First, a staged falsification
chain plus a targeted higher-statistics re-test shows that a
low-statistics robust classification can fail under increased Monte Carlo
averaging. It also shows that whether a cross-ansatz robust classification
survives that re-test is
\emph{convention-contingent}. The same procedure returns no surviving
candidate in a stray-field Zeeman bookkeeping, and two grid-local
robust survivors (edge pocket, $v=10$\,m/s, reduced magnet--well
separation, robust in all four ans\"atze at $n_{\rm real}=30$) in the physically preferred
total-field convention.
This offers a
template for guarding against premature ``sweet-spot'' claims in
co-design studies more generally, while making explicit that the
verdict itself carries modeling risk. Second, the atlas separates two notions of
model dependence: the ordering of conditions and their physical
classification. This localizes \emph{where} modeling uncertainty
matters most. Magnitude rankings are largely conserved across
ans\"atze, while two-channel sign assignments are poorly conserved,
particularly in the A--B$_x$ and B$_z$--B$_x$ comparisons. Co-design conclusions drawn
from a single phenomenological $H_{sv}$ ansatz and a single convention are
therefore not trustworthy
unless checked against alternative coupling descriptions and against
absolute full-model error proxies.

The practical message for valley-aware co-design is therefore one of
sequencing. In the model-uncertain setting examined here, geometry and
trajectory optimization alone cannot settle the co-design verdict while the
coupling form remains uncertain at the level we find here; effort is
better spent first constraining the spin--valley coupling against
microscopic theory or experiment. The atlas and its six metrics are
designed to be reused for exactly this purpose. They accept additional
coupling descriptions, including microscopically derived ones, as these
become available, and report whether the spread we observe narrows.

\paragraph{Future work.}
The natural next step is to replace the phenomenological coupling
ansatz with a microscopic effective $H_{sv}$ derived from orbital
admixture, valley--orbit interaction, and $g$-factor
anisotropy~\cite{friesen_theory_2010,hosseinkhani_theory_2022,zhang_controlling_2021}. Related
gradient, strain, and spin--orbit design questions in Ge hole-spin
platforms~\cite{sarkar_effect_2025,mauro_strain_2025,geyer_anisotropic_2024,hendrickx_sweet-spot_2024,jin_probing_2024}
can inform the same program.
Because our framework treats the coupling form as an interchangeable
module, such a microscopic model can be added as an additional entry
in the same atlas and compared pairwise against the toy families.
We expect this to narrow the model dependence identified here. Residual uncertainty in
microscopic input parameters will likely leave a finite degree of model
dependence. Calibrating the gradient structure of the synthetic
coupling against microscopic theory or experiment, rather than
refining the optimizer, is the decisive next step toward quantitative
valley-aware co-design.
